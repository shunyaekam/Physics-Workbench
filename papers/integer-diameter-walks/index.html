At every integer diameter from two up, a cycle can visit every site in a fullest disk.

The maximum number of steps equals the maximum number of sites among all disk placements. The only exceptional graph is too wide for diameters two and three. From diameter four onward, a competing disk already holds 19 sites.

This proves the proposed equality for both integer sequences using the same published graph theorem as the record-walk result.

Statistical mechanics

Full disks and longest walks at integer diameters

For every integer n≥2n\ge2, the maximum segment count of a self-avoiding walk (cycles allowed) in a disk of diameter nn is the maximum number of triangular-lattice sites in such a disk. These are A122226 and A125852 in their published integer index. Walks · Point counts

A disk of fixed diameter contains at most finitely many sites uniformly in its center. This follows by packing open radius-1/21/2 circles at these sites and comparing areas within the concentric disk enlarged by 1/21/2. Some placement therefore achieves the largest integer site count. Its induced graph is 2-connected and linearly convex by the disk-graph argument for A346126. It consequently has a Hamiltonian cycle unless it is the 13-vertex Star of David. Reay and Zamfirescu’s theorem

For diameters 22 and 33, opposite tips of that exceptional graph are 232\sqrt3 apart, too far to fit. For integer diameters n≥4n\ge4 the disk centered at a lattice site already contains its center, six neighbors at distance one, six at 3\sqrt3, and six at two. A maximizing disk therefore has at least 19 sites, excluding the exception. Its Hamiltonian cycle realizes one segment per site. Every open walk of mm segments uses m+1m+1 distinct sites, and every simple cycle of mm segments uses mm distinct sites, giving the converse upper bound.

The conjecture about equality of the two integer-indexed sequences follows. Extending this statement to every real diameter would introduce a different question. The proof makes no assertion of priority for the integer-diameter corollary.
