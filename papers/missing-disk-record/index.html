Thirteen sites fit. A walk still tops out at twelve.

Grow a disk on the triangular lattice. At squared radius 3 it can first hold 13 sites. A 13-step walk through those sites would have to close. The six tips force a cycle that leaves the center out.

Two independent exact searches over all disk centers find the missing tenth point record. The ninth positive walk record is 14, refuting the proposed equality. Kittipassorn, Phetmak and Sriamorn independently tabulated the point count.

Statistical mechanics

The missing thirteenth site

The tenth strict record for the number of triangular-lattice sites in a freely placed closed disk is 13, at squared radius 3. The ninth positive record for self-avoiding walk length is 14, at squared radius 13/4. The proposed equality of these two records for every index greater than two fails at index ten. The displayed table of point records goes directly from 12 sites to 14. Source entry and displayed table · Walk definition

Why the two counts separate

Take unit basis vectors meeting at 60 degrees. The squared norm of axial coordinates is Q(x,y)=x2+xy+y2Q(x,y)=x^2+xy+y^2. At a lattice site, a disk of squared radius 3 contains the center, six nearest neighbors and six outer tips. To visit all 13 sites in 13 unit steps, a self-avoiding walk would have to close. Each outer tip has exactly two possible neighbors. A cycle through the six tips is forced to use their twelve edges. These edges already form a cycle that misses the center. The longest permissible walk on this placement has twelve steps.

To refute a conjecture about the best possible disk, one must also allow other centers and establish where the records fall. Here are all the changes through squared radius 13/4. Walk length counts edges. A closed walk may repeat its starting vertex only at the end.

How the continuum of centers is exhausted

Any finite set with at least two points has a smallest enclosing disk. Its center lies in the convex hull of the boundary points, since otherwise one could move it toward their convex hull and reduce the radius. Thus some diametrically opposite pair, or some three noncollinear boundary points, determine that disk. Translate one boundary site to the origin. Every covered site then has squared norm at most four times the squared radius, hence at most 13 in this calculation. Since Q(x,y)≥3x2/4Q(x,y)\ge 3x^2/4 and Q(x,y)≥3y2/4Q(x,y)\ge 3y^2/4, both integer coordinates lie between −4 and 4. Singletons contribute the radius-zero case.

The replay enumerates all pair midpoints and noncollinear triple circumcenters in that box, using exact fractions, then collects every site inside each resulting disk. Extra circumcenters are harmless because they describe feasible disks. Any feasible walk or point set can be enclosed by its own minimum disk, so every first attainment of either record occurs among the enumerated disks. For each induced unit-neighbor graph the replay enumerates simple paths by their starting vertex, current vertex and visited set. A return edge to the start adds one step when at least three distinct vertices have been visited. Taking the maxima and keeping only strict increases gives the table above. No estimate of a floating-point boundary enters the test.

Independent checks and prior work

Splitting the triangular lattice into three colors gives a geometric proof that no smaller disk can hold 13 sites. The independent replay also enumerates the centers inside a single fundamental cell, using a different coordinate system. Three-color argument · Separate implementation
